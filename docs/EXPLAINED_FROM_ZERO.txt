--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
1. CRYPTOGRAPHY IN 10 MINUTES
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Cryptography is the science of scrambling information so that only the right
person can read it, and of proving that a message really came from who it claims.

Three jobs it does:

  (a) CONFIDENTIALITY — "nobody else can read this."
      Example: when you open https://bank.com, your browser and the bank agree
      on a secret key and scramble everything in between. A person watching the
      wire sees noise.

  (b) AUTHENTICITY / SIGNATURES — "this really came from me and was not changed."
      Example: when your phone installs an app update, it checks a digital
      signature. If even one byte was tampered with, the signature fails and the
      update is rejected.

  (c) KEY EXCHANGE — "let us two agree on a shared secret while a stranger is
      listening, without them learning it." This is the magic step that makes
      (a) possible over the open internet.

TWO FAMILIES OF ALGORITHMS

  SYMMETRIC crypto — one shared secret key used to both lock and unlock.
     Fast. Used for the bulk data.
     Main example: AES. Hash functions (SHA-256) are related tools that make a
     fixed-size "fingerprint" of data.

  ASYMMETRIC / PUBLIC-KEY crypto — a key PAIR: a public key everyone can see and
     a private key you keep secret. Anyone can lock with your public key; only
     you can unlock with your private key. Also used for signatures (you sign
     with the private key, the world verifies with the public key).
     Main examples: RSA, ECC (Elliptic Curve Cryptography), Diffie-Hellman (DH).

WHY PUBLIC-KEY CRYPTO IS SAFE TODAY
  Its security rests on maths problems that ordinary computers cannot solve in
  any reasonable time:
     - RSA: "factor this 617-digit number into its two prime factors."
     - ECC / DH: "solve this discrete logarithm."
  A normal computer would need billions of years. So we trust it.

WHERE IT ALL LIVES
  RSA and ECC are underneath almost everything: HTTPS, UPI, Aadhaar, net-banking,
  VPNs, WhatsApp, software updates, government PKI, SSH logins, code signing,
  TLS certificates. If you could break RSA and ECC, you could read "secure"
  traffic and forge "trusted" signatures across the whole digital world.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
2. THE QUANTUM THREAT
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

A quantum computer is a fundamentally different kind of machine. It is not "a
faster laptop." It uses quantum physics to explore many possibilities at once
for certain specific problems.

In 1994 a mathematician named Peter Shor published an algorithm. On a
large enough quantum computer, Shor's algorithm solves EXACTLY the maths
problems that RSA, ECC, DH, DSA and ECDSA rely on — quickly.

  Consequence:
     RSA, ECC, Diffie-Hellman, DSA, ECDSA  ->  COMPLETELY BROKEN.
     Not "weaker." Broken. The private key can be computed from the public key.
     A signature can be forged. A recorded "secure" channel can be decrypted.

  Symmetric crypto is only DENTED, not broken:
     A different quantum algorithm (Grover's) roughly halves the strength of
     AES and hashes. The fix is easy and cheap: use AES-256 instead of AES-128,
     use SHA-384/512 instead of SHA-256. No redesign needed.

So the quantum problem is really a PUBLIC-KEY problem: RSA, ECC and DH have to be
replaced. Symmetric crypto just needs bigger sizes.

DOES THE QUANTUM COMPUTER EXIST YET?
  No — not one big enough. Today's machines have a few hundred noisy qubits.
  Breaking RSA-2048 needs millions of high-quality qubits. Most expert
  estimates put a "cryptographically-relevant quantum computer" (CRQC) in the
  early-to-mid 2030s, but this is genuinely uncertain. It could be later. It
  could, with a breakthrough, be sooner.

  IMPORTANT: the exact date does not actually matter much. Read the next
  section to see why.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
3. "HARVEST NOW, DECRYPT LATER" — WHY THIS IS A PROBLEM TODAY
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Here is the sentence that makes this urgent:

   An adversary can RECORD your encrypted traffic today, store it cheaply for
   years, and DECRYPT it later once they have a quantum computer.

This is called "Harvest Now, Decrypt Later" (HNDL), or "store now, decrypt
later." Nation-state adversaries are widely assumed to be doing it already —
storage is cheap, and encrypted data is worth keeping.

So ask: how long does my data need to stay secret?
   - A one-time password: 30 seconds. Nobody cares.
   - A news article: already public.
   - Health records, defence communications, financial data, biometric data,
     state secrets, long-term contracts: 10, 15, 25 years.

Any data with a long secrecy lifetime that is protected by RSA/ECC key exchange
TODAY is ALREADY effectively exposed — even though the quantum computer is years
away. That data is being harvested now.

That is why "we have until 2032" is the wrong way to think. If your data must
stay secret for 15 years and migration takes 3 years, you are already too late.
(That is Mosca's inequality — section 6.)


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
4. THE FIX: POST-QUANTUM CRYPTOGRAPHY (PQC)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Post-quantum cryptography = new public-key algorithms built on DIFFERENT maths
problems that are believed to be hard for BOTH normal computers AND quantum
computers (mostly "lattice" problems and hash-based constructions).

The US standards body NIST ran an 8-year worldwide competition and, in August
2024, published the finished standards:

  STANDARD    NAME       (was called)   WHAT IT REPLACES
  --------    ----       -------------   ----------------
  FIPS 203    ML-KEM     Kyber           Key exchange  (replaces RSA/ECDH key transport)
  FIPS 204    ML-DSA     Dilithium       Signatures    (replaces RSA/ECDSA signing)
  FIPS 205    SLH-DSA    SPHINCS+        Signatures    (conservative hash-based backup)

  Also in the pipeline: FN-DSA (Falcon), HQC, and the stateful hash-based
  signature schemes XMSS / LMS for firmware.

India's National Quantum Mission has ADOPTED these same NIST algorithms.

HYBRID MODE
  During the transition, many systems run a classical algorithm AND a PQC
  algorithm together (e.g. X25519 + ML-KEM-768 in TLS). If either one is later
  found weak, the other still protects you. CRYPTONEX recommends hybrid where
  the target library supports it.

"CRYPTO-AGILITY"
  A system is "crypto-agile" if you can swap its algorithm WITHOUT rebuilding
  the system — the algorithm is a setting, not hard-wired. Most legacy systems
  are NOT agile: "RSA" is typed directly into the code in a hundred places.
  Lack of agility is the real reason migration takes years.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
5. THE CATCH: YOU CANNOT FIX WHAT YOU CANNOT FIND
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The new algorithms exist and are ready. So why is this hard?

Because a bank, a power utility, or a government department has THOUSANDS of
servers, applications, code repositories, libraries, certificates, hardware
security modules and hard-coded crypto calls — built over 20 years by people who
have left.

   Nobody has a list of where cryptography is used.

Every PQC migration plan in the world therefore starts with the SAME first step:

   BUILD A CRYPTOGRAPHIC INVENTORY.

In standard form, that inventory is called a:

   CBOM — Cryptographic Bill of Materials.

Think of the "ingredients label" on a food packet. An SBOM (Software Bill of
Materials) lists the software packages in a product. A CBOM lists the
CRYPTOGRAPHY: every algorithm, key size, certificate, protocol and library,
and where each one is used. There is an open standard for the file format,
called CycloneDX CBOM.

CRYPTONEX's core job is: produce that inventory automatically, then analyse it.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
6. MOSCA'S INEQUALITY — THE ONE FORMULA TO KNOW
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Michele Mosca's rule for "should I be worried about this piece of data?":

        X   +   Y   >   Z          ->   you are already too late
        |       |       |
        |       |       +--  Z = years until a quantum computer can break RSA/ECC
        |       +----------  Y = years it will take you to migrate this system
        +------------------  X = years this data must stay secret

  Worked example:
     Health records must stay secret 15 years   -> X = 15
     Migrating this hospital system takes 3 yrs  -> Y = 3
     Quantum computer estimated in ~8 years      -> Z = 8
     15 + 3 = 18  which is GREATER THAN  8
     -> This data is ALREADY exposed to harvest-now-decrypt-later.

The point of Mosca is NOT to predict a date. Z is uncertain and that is fine.
The point is RELATIVE PRIORITISATION: out of 5,000 systems, which 40 do I fix
first? The ones where X + Y most exceeds Z.

In CRYPTONEX, X, Y and Z are adjustable inputs (sliders / presets), never a
hard-coded date. You run scenarios.

  Dec 2026   Quantum-safe certification labs operational
  Dec 2027   COMPLETE CRYPTOGRAPHIC INVENTORIES across defence, power,
             telecom and BFSI (banking / financial services / insurance)
  Dec 2028   Migrate high-priority systems
  Dec 2029   Full PQC adoption

Other governments have parallel deadlines: US (NSM-10, CISA/NIST), EU (2030 for
financial infrastructure).

So: tens of thousands of Indian organisations legally need EXACTLY the inventory
CRYPTONEX produces, about 18 months from now. Today, doing it needs expensive
foreign tools. That gap is the opportunity.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
9. WHAT WE DECIDED TO BUILD — CRYPTONEX
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

CRYPTONEX is a free, fully OFFLINE command-line tool and local web console that:

   1. DISCOVERS every cryptographic asset in a codebase — across source code,
      dependency manifests, certificates and keys, container images and
      compiled binaries.

   2. ASSESSES each asset — is it quantum-vulnerable? how business-critical is
      it? is it exposed to harvest-now-decrypt-later? does it fail Mosca's
      inequality? — and rolls this up into an estate-wide posture grade.

   3. RECOMMENDS a specific fix for each unsafe asset — the exact NIST PQC
      algorithm, a hybrid transition option, the migration effort, and which
      libraries support it — and sequences everything into a migration plan
      mapped to India's NQM deadlines.

   4. EXPORTS the result as a CycloneDX 1.6 CBOM, a SARIF file for CI/CD, raw
      JSON, and a human-readable HTML/PDF report.

ONE-LINE PITCH:
   "You cannot migrate what you cannot see. CRYPTONEX discovers your
    cryptographic footprint, assesses its quantum exposure, and turns it into
    an actionable migration plan — free, offline, and in one scan."

WHY "OFFLINE" MATTERS:
   A defence or power-grid cryptographic inventory is itself sensitive — it is a
   map of where the locks are. You cannot send that to a foreign cloud service.
   CRYPTONEX never makes a network call. Nothing is uploaded.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
10. HOW CRYPTONEX WORKS — THE 3-STEP PIPELINE
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

        +-----------+        +-----------+        +--------------+
        | DISCOVER  |  --->  |  ASSESS   |  --->  |  RECOMMEND   |
        +-----------+        +-----------+        +--------------+
        6 scanners           quantum status       PQC algorithm
        read the codebase    + risk score         + hybrid option
        and produce a list   + criticality        + effort + libs
        of crypto assets     + Mosca verdict      + migration wave
                             + posture grade      + NQM phase

  DISCOVER: six independent "collectors" (scanners) each look at one kind of
     artefact and emit crypto assets with evidence (file, line, snippet).

  ASSESS: a pure-logic engine (no I/O, fully testable) takes each asset and:
     - looks up its quantum status in a versioned knowledge base;
     - infers business criticality from path and config signals;
     - infers data classification (PII / PCI / SECRET / INTERNAL ...);
     - checks harvest-now-decrypt-later exposure;
     - applies Mosca's inequality with the current X / Y / Z;
     - computes a 0-100 risk score;
     - rolls all assets up into a posture score and an A-F grade.

  RECOMMEND: for every unsafe asset, a rule-based engine matches it against a
     PQC mapping table and returns a concrete target algorithm, a hybrid
     transition, the migration effort class, latency notes, and supporting
     libraries. Assets are then grouped into migration WAVES by effort and
     mapped to NQM phases.

DESIGN PRINCIPLE — "KNOWLEDGE AS DATA":
  All the cryptographic knowledge (which algorithms are broken, which library
  versions have CVEs, which PQC algorithm replaces what) lives in versioned
  YAML files, NOT in code. Updating knowledge = editing a data file, and the
  CBOM records which knowledge-base version produced it.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
11. FEATURE BY FEATURE — EVERY PART, WHAT IT IS, WHAT IT DOES
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

11.1  THE SIX SCANNERS (the DISCOVER step)
------------------------------------------

  1. SOURCE SCANNER
     WHAT: reads source code in 12 languages (Python, Java, JavaScript, C#,
           Rust, PHP, Ruby, Swift, Go, C/C++, plus config and shell) using
           ~162 pattern rules.
     FINDS: calls to crypto APIs and primitives — "this file calls RSA key
           generation", "this uses AES in ECB mode", "this hashes with MD5",
           "this creates an Ed25519 signing key". Captures the file, the line
           and the code snippet as evidence.

  2. DEPENDENCY SCANNER
     WHAT: reads dependency manifests across 9 ecosystems (pip / npm / Maven /
           Gradle / Cargo / Go modules / Composer / RubyGems / NuGet).
     FINDS: crypto libraries and their exact versions, then cross-references
           an advisories file (13 known CVE / RUSTSEC / GHSA entries) to raise
           a "vulnerable-dependency" finding when a version is in a bad range.
           Example: "pyjwt 1.7.1 — key-confusion when `algorithms` is not
           pinned (CVE)."

  3. CERTIFICATE SCANNER
     WHAT: parses X.509 certificates and key files (.crt, .pem, .der ...).
     FINDS: the signature algorithm, key algorithm and size, validity dates,
           whether it is a CA (certificate authority) key, whether it is
           self-signed. For privacy it stores only a SHA-256 fingerprint of
           the public key, never the key itself. Hardened so real-world test
           certificates in open-source repos do not crash it.

  4. MISUSE / THREAT SCANNER
     WHAT: 28 rules for INSECURE USE of cryptography (as opposed to just
           "old algorithm").
     FINDS: hard-coded keys and secrets, AES in ECB mode, static / zero IVs,
           `verify=False` (TLS verification disabled), `InsecureSkipVerify`,
           JWT "alg: none", weak SSH KexAlgorithms/Ciphers/MACs, non-constant-
           time comparison of secrets, MD5/SHA1 for security, and HAND-ROLLED
           CRYPTO — it fingerprints 28 known crypto constants (S-boxes, round
           constants, magic numbers) to catch someone re-implementing AES, DES,
           MD5 etc. by hand inside their own code.

  5. CONTAINER-IMAGE SCANNER
     WHAT: unpacks an OCI / `docker save` image tar, merges the layers
           (respecting file deletions / "whiteouts"), with zip-bomb guards.
     FINDS: it then runs the source / dependency / certificate / misuse
           scanners over the merged filesystem, tagging every finding with the
           image path (`image.tar!/app/...`). Also flags secret-looking
           environment variables baked into the image.

  6. BINARY SCANNER
     WHAT: uses the `lief` library to inspect compiled executables and shared
           libraries (ELF / PE / Mach-O).
     FINDS: linked crypto libraries, crypto-related symbols and strings —
           "this binary is linked against an old OpenSSL", "this embeds a
           certificate."

  Every scanner is wrapped in error handling: if one fails on a weird file, the
  scan still completes and the failure is recorded as a coverage limitation
  (honesty over a fake "100% success").

11.2  THE KNOWLEDGE BASE (versioned YAML — "knowledge as data")
--------------------------------------------------------------
     - algorithms.yaml   : 42 algorithm families, each with its primitive
                           (signature / kem / hash / block-cipher ...), quantum
                           status, and reasoning text.
     - aliases.yaml      : 108 alternate names -> canonical family
                           (e.g. "rsaEncryption", "RSA-PSS" -> RSA).
     - libraries.yaml    : 80 crypto libraries, with end-of-life flags.
     - advisories.yaml   : 13 CVE / RUSTSEC / GHSA entries with affected
                           version ranges.
     - pqc_mapping.yaml  : 9 rules mapping an unsafe algorithm + context to a
                           PQC recommendation.
     - policy.yaml       : data-classification keyword hints, criticality
                           weights.
     - constants.yaml    : 28 crypto-constant fingerprints for hand-rolled
                           detection.
     - misuse.yaml       : 28 misuse rules.
     - rules/*.yaml      : per-language detection rules (12 languages).
     KB version is stamped into every report (currently "2026.02").

11.3  THE QUANTUM-STATUS CLASSIFIER
----------------------------------
     Every asset gets one of:
        SAFE       - quantum-resistant at current parameters (e.g. AES-256,
                     ML-KEM, SHA-384).
        WEAKENED   - survives but needs a bigger size (e.g. AES-128 -> AES-256).
        VULNERABLE - broken by Shor's algorithm (RSA, ECC, DH, ECDSA, DSA).
        BROKEN     - already broken even without quantum (MD5, SHA-1, DES, RC4).
        UNKNOWN    - could not classify; needs human review.
     This is a DETERMINISTIC lookup with a citation, not a guess.

11.4  RISK SCORING (0-100 per asset)
-----------------------------------
     A transparent, published formula. In words, the risk of an asset goes UP
     when:
        - its quantum status is worse (broken > vulnerable > weakened > safe);
        - its business criticality is higher (critical > high > medium > low);
        - its Mosca "exposure" (how many years X+Y exceeds Z-now) is larger;
        - it is external-facing rather than internal;
        - the detection confidence is higher (we don't inflate risk on a guess);
        - it is exposed to harvest-now-decrypt-later (x1.15 multiplier).
     Every asset in the console can show its exact score breakdown.

11.5  POSTURE SCORE & GRADE (the estate headline)
------------------------------------------------
     All PRODUCTION assets (test-fixture code is excluded from grading) are
     combined into a single 0-100 posture score and an A-F letter grade:
        A >= 85   B >= 70   C >= 55   D >= 40   F < 40
     This is the number a manager reads first. Example demo result:
     "46 assets, 25 weaknesses, posture B (79/100)."

11.6  HNDL (HARVEST-NOW-DECRYPT-LATER) DETECTION
-----------------------------------------------
     Flags any asset whose primitive is a CONFIDENTIALITY primitive
     (public-key encryption, key agreement, or KEM) that is exposed today —
     because that is the traffic an adversary records now to decrypt later.
     Signatures are less HNDL-sensitive (a forged signature in 2035 is less
     useful than 2035-decrypted 2026 secrets). There is also an "HNDL at rest"
     count for long-lived encrypted stored data.

11.7  CRYPTO-AGILITY INDEX (0-100)
---------------------------------
     Estimates how EASY migration will be, based on how concentrated vs
     scattered the crypto usage is, whether it goes through wrappers/config or
     is hard-coded everywhere, and library support. A low agility index means
     "this will hurt" even if the algorithms themselves are simple to name.

11.8  MIGRATION WAVES
--------------------
     Unsafe assets are grouped into ordered waves by EFFORT:
        Wave 1  drop-in       (just bump a library / flip a config)
        Wave 2  config        (protocol / parameter change)
        Wave 3  code          (application code changes)
        Wave 4  architectural (roots of trust, HSM, cross-signing)
        Wave 5  vendor / external dependency
     Each wave shows asset count, risk removed, and examples. This turns a flat
     list into a plan someone can actually schedule.

11.9  NQM PHASE MAPPING
----------------------
     Every unsafe asset is tagged against India's National Quantum Mission
     timeline: "high-priority-2028", "full-adoption-2029", or "no-action"
     (already safe). So the plan is expressed in the language of the national
     mandate, not just internal jargon.

11.10 QUANTUM-RISK TIMELINE
--------------------------
     A small chart: "if a cryptographically-relevant quantum computer arrives
     in year N, how many of your assets fail Mosca's inequality?" — plotted for
     a range of N. Shows the risk is not a cliff on one date; it grows.

11.11 MOSCA LAB (interactive)
----------------------------
     A dedicated console page with three sliders (X = data secrecy lifetime,
     Y = migration time, Z = quantum threat year) and presets
     (NIST baseline / Optimistic / Regulator-EU-2030). Move a slider and the
     ENTIRE estate re-scores live — posture grade, at-risk count, HNDL count.
     This is how you answer "your Z is a guess": you don't defend a date, you
     let the organisation model their own and watch the effect.

11.12 THE RECOMMENDATION ENGINE (the RECOMMEND step)
--------------------------------------------------
     For each unsafe asset, matches it against pqc_mapping.yaml and returns:
        - TARGET ALGORITHM        e.g. RSA signing key -> ML-DSA-65;
                                       long-lived root CA -> SLH-DSA-SHA2-192s;
                                       RSA/ECDH key transport -> ML-KEM-768.
        - HYBRID / TRANSITION     e.g. "X25519 + ML-KEM-768" where supported.
        - MIGRATION EFFORT        drop-in / config / code / architectural.
        - LATENCY CLASS + LIBS    which real libraries support the target
                                  (OpenSSL 3.5+, liboqs, BouncyCastle ...).
        - RATIONALE               one plain sentence of why.
     Returns nothing for SAFE / UNKNOWN assets (no busywork).

11.13 THE EVIDENCE / INFERENCE / ASSUMPTION MODEL  (the credibility layer)
------------------------------------------------------------------------
     This is what makes CRYPTONEX defensible in front of a sceptical expert.
     Every asset's assessment is split into four clearly-labelled buckets:

        OBSERVED    - evidence-backed FACTS. "certificate collector, confidence
                      CONFIRMED, RSA-4096, CA:TRUE, subject CN=..., here is the
                      file it came from."
        KNOWLEDGE BASE - deterministic, cited lookups. "RSA is VULNERABLE —
                      Shor's algorithm recovers the private key by factoring
                      the modulus."
        INFERRED    - HEURISTICS, shown with their reasoning. "criticality =
                      critical because the path contains 'pki' (+22), it is a
                      root CA (+15), it is external-facing (+30)..." A human
                      confirms these; we never present them as organisational
                      truth.
        ASSUMED     - operator-set scenario inputs. "X = 20y, Y = 5y, Z = 2032
                      -> exposed ~19 years." These are YOUR assumptions, not
                      our predictions.

     The tool NEVER blurs "we saw this" with "we think this." Test-fixture code
     is also separated from production code so a repo full of test vectors does
     not tank the grade.

11.14 THREAT FINDINGS (crypto misuse) — separate from the quantum inventory
-------------------------------------------------------------------------
     The "Weaknesses" view lists insecure USE of cryptography found by the
     misuse scanner: disabled TLS verification, hard-coded secrets, ECB mode,
     JWT alg:none, weak DH groups, hand-rolled crypto, and so on — each with a
     severity, a CWE id, the location, the code snippet, and a fix. These are
     "you have a problem RIGHT NOW," independent of quantum.

11.15 OUTPUTS
------------
     - cbom.json      CycloneDX 1.6 Cryptographic Bill of Materials. Includes
                      cryptoProperties, a vulnerabilities array, a deterministic
                      serial number, and namespaced `cryptonex:*` properties
                      (detection confidence, criticality basis, etc.). Feeds
                      into whatever platform the organisation adopts later.
     - results.sarif  SARIF 2.1.0 — standard static-analysis format. Drop it
                      into GitHub / GitLab CI to get pull-request annotations
                      and a merge gate on new vulnerable assets in critical
                      paths.
     - result.json    the complete raw ScanResult for a SIEM / data lake.
     - report.html    executive + audit report: posture grade, top risks, Mosca
                      summary, per-critical-asset detail with the evidence-vs-
                      assessment split, migration plan by wave. Converts to PDF
                      if weasyprint is present.
     CBOM output is DETERMINISTIC — same input, same bytes — so it can be
     diffed in CI.

11.17 OFFLINE / PRIVACY
----------------------
     Zero network calls. The scan runs on your machine. The cryptographic
     inventory — a sensitive document — never leaves the building. The Docker
     image runs as a non-root user. This is a hard requirement for defence,
     power and government use and a key differentiator vs the cloud tools.

12. WHAT WE DID NOT BUILD (and why that is fine)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The PS lists "HSMs and cloud crypto services" among the things to scan. We did
NOT build those two collectors, on purpose — the PS itself says to mark them as
roadmap and avoid scope creep. They are documented in docs/ROADMAP_HSM_CLOUD.md
with the exact approach:

  - HSM collector: PKCS#11 interface via python-pkcs11, tested against SoftHSM2.
    Would enumerate keys, mechanisms and their quantum status inside a hardware
    security module.
  - Cloud collector: read-only calls to AWS KMS / ACM, Azure Key Vault, GCP KMS
    (using boto3 etc., mocked with `moto` for tests). Would inventory managed
    keys, their algorithms and rotation state.

Also "live network / TLS endpoint sweep" is roadmap (tools like SandboxAQ took
years on this). Everything the PS calls the core — source, dependencies,
certificates, container images, plus binaries as a bonus — is built and working.

16. JUDGE QUESTIONS & HONEST ANSWERS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Q: "Do quantum computers even exist?"
A: Small noisy ones do (IBM, Google — a few hundred qubits). Breaking RSA-2048
   needs millions of good qubits; estimates say early-to-mid 2030s. But because
   of harvest-now-decrypt-later, and because migration takes years, it is a
   TODAY problem regardless of the exact date.

Q: "Isn't this just an antivirus / SBOM tool?"
A: An SBOM lists software packages. A CBOM lists cryptographic assets
   specifically — algorithms, key sizes, certificates, protocols. And we go
   further than a list: quantum risk scoring, Mosca with your business context,
   and the specific replacement algorithm.

Q: "Doesn't IBM already make this?"
A: IBM and SandboxAQ do — ~$100k+/yr enterprise contracts, delivered as foreign
   cloud services. You cannot send a defence cryptographic inventory to a
   foreign SaaS. Each covers only part of the surface (IBM code, SandboxAQ
   network). The one free tool, cbomkit, does 3 languages of source code and
   stops at a flat list. CRYPTONEX is free, offline, unified and actionable.

Q: "How do I know your scanner isn't full of false positives?"
A: Two answers. One — every finding carries a stated detection confidence and
   its evidence (file, line, snippet), verifiable in seconds. Two — we
   benchmarked against 7 real open-source projects (PyJWT, paramiko,
   python-jose, pyca/cryptography, golang-jwt, node-jsonwebtoken, itsdangerous);
   docs/benchmark has the results and a precision spot-check sheet. Scans run in
   under 6 seconds, and we separate production findings from test-fixture ones.

Q: "How do you know this crypto actually protects sensitive data?"
A: We don't claim to. CRYPTONEX separates OBSERVED evidence from INFERRED risk.
   The algorithm, parameters and location are observed. Business criticality,
   data classification and external exposure are heuristic inferences from path
   and config signals — labelled as such, with the reasoning shown. They are a
   prioritisation starting point for a human, not organisational truth.

Q: "Why 2032? Who decides X, Y, Z?"
A: Nobody in the tool. X, Y and Z are configurable. The Mosca Lab ships
   NIST-baseline, optimistic and EU-2030-regulator presets and lets an
   organisation model their own. We don't predict a date; we run a transparent
   scenario and re-score the estate live.

Q: "What's the 'AI' in it?"
A: Pattern-recognition scanning that can flag crypto usage it hasn't seen an
   exact rule for, and the analysis engine that reasons from an asset's risk
   profile to a prioritised recommendation. Plus an optional local model to
   explain each finding in plain English. The core value is deterministic and
   auditable — which is what you want in a compliance tool.

Q: "Can you really scan every language / every system?"
A: The prototype covers source in 12 languages, dependency manifests in 9
   ecosystems, certificate and key files, container images, and binaries —
   already broader than the free alternative. Live network sweeps and
   hardware-module integration are roadmap; those took the commercial vendors
   years.

Q: "Does Grover's algorithm break AES?"
A: No. It gives a quadratic speedup on brute force — effectively halves the key
   strength. AES-128 -> ~64-bit effective, so move to AES-256, which stays
   safe. Same idea for hashes: use SHA-384/512.

17. GLOSSARY — every term, one line
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

  AES               Advanced Encryption Standard. The standard symmetric cipher.
                    AES-256 is quantum-safe; AES-128 is "weakened."
  Asymmetric crypto See "public-key crypto."
  BFSI              Banking, Financial Services and Insurance (a regulated sector).
  Binary            A compiled program or library (not source code).
  CA                Certificate Authority — an entity whose key signs other
                    certificates. A "root CA" is the top of the trust chain.
  CBOM              Cryptographic Bill of Materials — a structured inventory of
                    all cryptography in a system. Standard format: CycloneDX CBOM.
  CI / CD           Continuous Integration / Delivery — automated build+test
                    pipelines. SARIF output plugs into these.
  CRQC              Cryptographically-Relevant Quantum Computer — one big enough
                    to break RSA-2048. Does not exist yet.
  Crypto-agility    The ability to change algorithm without rebuilding the system.
  CVE               Common Vulnerabilities and Exposures — a public ID for a
                    known security flaw (e.g. CVE-2022-xxxxx).
  CWE               Common Weakness Enumeration — a category ID for a TYPE of
                    flaw (e.g. CWE-327 "broken crypto algorithm").
  CycloneDX         An open standard for bills of materials; has a CBOM profile.
  DH / Diffie-Hellman  A key-exchange algorithm. Quantum-vulnerable.
  DSA / ECDSA       Digital Signature Algorithm / its elliptic-curve version.
                    Quantum-vulnerable.
  ECC               Elliptic Curve Cryptography — compact public-key crypto used
                    everywhere (e.g. Ed25519, P-256). Quantum-vulnerable.
  ECB mode          A weak way of using a block cipher — identical input blocks
                    give identical output blocks. A misuse finding.
  FIPS 203/204/205  The three finalised NIST PQC standards (ML-KEM, ML-DSA,
                    SLH-DSA).
  Grover's algorithm  A quantum algorithm that speeds up brute-force search;
                    halves symmetric-key strength. Fix: bigger keys.
  Hash function     Makes a fixed-size fingerprint of data (e.g. SHA-256).
                    MD5 and SHA-1 are broken.
  HNDL              Harvest Now, Decrypt Later — record encrypted data today,
                    decrypt it after a quantum computer arrives.
  HSM               Hardware Security Module — a tamper-resistant box that stores
                    keys and does crypto operations. (Collector is roadmap.)
  Hybrid crypto     Running a classical + a PQC algorithm together during the
                    transition, so you're safe if either is later broken.
  IV                Initialisation Vector — a per-message random value a cipher
                    needs. A static or zero IV is a misuse finding.
  JWT               JSON Web Token — a signed token used for logins/APIs.
                    "alg: none" disables the signature — a critical misuse.
  KEM               Key Encapsulation Mechanism — the modern name for a
                    key-exchange primitive. ML-KEM is the PQC KEM.
  Key exchange      Two parties agree on a shared secret over an open channel.
  Knowledge base    CRYPTONEX's versioned YAML files holding all crypto facts.
  Lattice crypto    The maths family most PQC algorithms are built on; believed
                    hard for quantum computers.
  lief              A Python library for parsing binaries (ELF/PE/Mach-O).
  ML-DSA            "Module-Lattice Digital Signature Algorithm" (was Dilithium).
                    FIPS 204. Replaces RSA/ECDSA signatures.
  ML-KEM            "Module-Lattice Key Encapsulation Mechanism" (was Kyber).
                    FIPS 203. Replaces RSA/ECDH key exchange.
  Mosca's inequality  X (secrecy lifetime) + Y (migration time) > Z (years to a
                    quantum computer) => the data is already at risk.
  NIST              US National Institute of Standards and Technology — runs the
                    PQC standardisation.
  NQM               National Quantum Mission (India). Mandates crypto inventories
                    for critical sectors by Dec 2027.
  NTRO              National Technical Research Organisation — the PS 26164 org.
  OCI image         The standard format for a container image (what Docker
                    builds).
  PII / PCI         Personally Identifiable Information / Payment Card Industry
                    data — data-classification labels CRYPTONEX infers.
  PKI               Public Key Infrastructure — the system of CAs and
                    certificates that underpins trust on the internet.
  Post-quantum / PQC  Cryptography designed to resist quantum computers.
  Posture score     CRYPTONEX's 0-100 estate headline, graded A-F.
  Primitive         The category of a crypto operation: signature, KEM,
                    key-agreement, public-key-encryption, block-cipher, hash...
  Private key       The secret half of a key pair. Must never leak.
  Public-key crypto  Crypto with a public + private key pair. RSA, ECC, DH.
                    This is the family the quantum threat breaks.
  Quantum computer  A machine using quantum physics; can run Shor's / Grover's.
  RSA               The classic public-key algorithm (named after its inventors).
                    Security = "factoring is hard." Quantum-vulnerable.
  SARIF             Static Analysis Results Interchange Format — a standard JSON
                    format for code-scan results; CI systems understand it.
  SBOM              Software Bill of Materials — inventory of software packages
                    (the non-crypto cousin of a CBOM).
  Shor's algorithm  The 1994 quantum algorithm that breaks RSA, ECC, DH, DSA,
                    ECDSA on a large enough quantum computer.
  SLH-DSA           "Stateless Hash-based DSA" (was SPHINCS+). FIPS 205. A
                    conservative signature scheme for long-lived roots of trust.
  Static analysis   Examining code/artefacts WITHOUT running them. What the
                    scanners do.
  Symmetric crypto  One shared secret key. AES, hashes. Only "weakened" by
                    quantum, not broken.
  TLS               Transport Layer Security — the "S" in HTTPS.
  X.509             The standard format for a digital certificate.
  YAML              A human-readable data file format. CRYPTONEX's knowledge
                    base is YAML.
